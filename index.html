Brown places power with, power to, and power within in one column. Our focus here is shared influence: what people can shape with others. Those terms need not mean the same thing.

02 SOLIDARITY & CHARITY

Who defines the need?
Who shapes the response?

Charity often begins with an offer of help: someone has resources and wants to meet another person’s need. Solidarity asks how people can work together to shape the response and change the conditions behind that need. The distinction turns on who defines the problem, who makes decisions, and whose knowledge can change the work.

A CHARITY MINDSET

Providing for others

The people offering help may set the priorities, control the resources, and decide what success looks like. Recipients can receive support while having little say in its design.

Whose understanding of the problem guides the work?

A SOLIDARITY MINDSET

Working with others

People affected by a problem help define it, shape the response, and challenge the conditions that sustain it. Their knowledge can change the work itself.

What can affected people influence, revise, or refuse?

Keep the situation in view.

Immediate material help can matter. The question is how it is organized and whether people also have ways to influence the conditions affecting them. Calling a project “collaborative” does not establish that power is shared. Look for the actual decisions, resources, and possibilities for disagreement.

03 NOTICE THE PRACTICES

Power shows up
in ordinary choices.

Look at how a group makes decisions, shares resources, responds to disagreement, and learns from mistakes. These everyday practices can concentrate control or give more people meaningful influence. A title, deadline, or clear rule alone does not tell you how power operates.

01 / Decisions

Power over: A small group decides for everyone and treats other approaches as a threat to order.

Power with: People affected help shape choices, consider multiple paths, and build commitment through participation.

A question to test this distinction

Can people change a priority before it is settled, or only comment after the decision is final?

02 / Information & resources

Power over: Key information and resources are withheld or selectively distributed to preserve control.

Power with: People share what others need to understand the situation and contribute to the work.

A question to test this distinction

Who can see the budget, understand the constraints, and help decide what gets priority?

03 / Relationships

Power over: Fear, distrust, or indifference makes it costly to ask questions or challenge a decision.

Power with: People work to earn trust, show empathy, and recognize one another’s capacity to contribute.

A question to test this distinction

What happens when someone disagrees with the person who has more authority?

04 / Learning & accountability

Power over: A few people are treated as the source of knowledge; problems lead to blame.

Power with: People bring experience worth learning from; the group examines and improves its processes.

A question to test this distinction

Whose experience can change the group’s explanation of what went wrong?

Sharing influence does not remove limits on time, money, or responsibility. Examine which choices are open, which are constrained, and who has a say in setting those boundaries.

FICTIONAL EXAMPLE

A warm welcome.
A workshop that misses the need.

A campus group plans a free workshop for new students. Organizers choose the topic, time, and format before inviting participants. They welcome everyone warmly, but several participants say the time is difficult and the topic misses their main questions. The group has one room reservation and a limited budget.

Consider a possible revision

Organizers could share the limits and ask participants to help choose the workshop’s priority questions while those choices are still open. They could invite written input from people who cannot attend and explain which suggestions changed the plan.

This is a proposal, not an observed success. We would still need to ask who could participate, whose input influenced the result, and whether the revised workshop addressed their needs.

You may use this fictional example in any journal prompt. For Prompt 1, describe a pattern it could illustrate across settings and label it as a possibility. For Prompt 3, propose a power-with revision and identify effects you would check rather than claim they occurred.
