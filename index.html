01 READ

Connected. But who has a say?

Friday’s discussion of relational intelligence raised questions about how social media shapes relationships and how we learn through disagreement. These questions also help us notice power: who gets heard, whose interpretation shapes a problem, and who can influence what happens next.

FROM RELATIONAL INTELLIGENCE TO SHARED POWER

Carry these questions into the readings.

Consider the two fictional situations below. As you read, notice how connection, disagreement, and influence appear in each. Let the questions guide what you look for in Brown’s comparison, Illich’s speech, and the sections on solidarity and shared power.

A group chat: connection, pressure, or both?

A student team uses a group chat to coordinate a project. Some members exchange ideas throughout the day. Two members read the thread after work and find that the group has already settled on a plan. Everyone was included in the chat.

Where do you see connection? Where do you see influence? What would you need to know about the group’s decision process before deciding whether power is being shared?

AI advice: feeling heard and making room for disagreement

After a disagreement, a team member asks an AI tool for advice. The response affirms their account and suggests a way forward. The other person’s account is not included.

What might feel helpful about that response? What might be missing? If the team acts on this advice, whose interpretation gets to define the problem? What would you want to hear before making a decision?

As you read, think of a team, workplace, class, organization, or public situation you know. A leader may have a formal title or influence others through everyday actions. The same person can use power differently in different moments.

02 SOLIDARITY & CHARITY

Who defines the need?
Who shapes the response?

Charity offers help to meet a need. Solidarity asks people to work together to shape the response and address the conditions behind it. In either case, ask who defines the problem and who can change the plan.

A CHARITY MINDSET

Providing for others

Those offering help may control priorities and resources. Receiving support does not necessarily give someone a say in its design.

Whose understanding of the problem guides the work?

A SOLIDARITY MINDSET

Working with others

People affected help define the problem and shape the response. Their knowledge can change the work and the conditions it addresses.

What can affected people influence, revise, or refuse?

REQUIRED READING · IVAN ILLICH · 1968

To Hell with Good Intentions

Illich delivered this speech in Cuernavaca, Mexico, on April 20, 1968, to the Conference on InterAmerican Student Projects. Addressing U.S. student volunteers, he links their service ambitions to unequal power between the United States and Latin America.

His argument challenges the assumption that wanting to help gives someone the knowledge or authority to intervene. Read it as a forceful historical argument you can question. The speech includes dated language and racial slurs quoted in its criticism of U.S. racism.

03 NOTICE THE PRACTICES

Power shows up
in ordinary choices.

Use these comparisons to examine decisions, resources, disagreement, and learning. A title or rule alone does not tell you how power operates.

01Decisions

Power over: A small group decides for everyone and treats other approaches as a threat to order.

Power with: People affected help shape choices, consider multiple paths, and build commitment through participation.

A question to test this distinction

Can people change a priority before it is settled, or only comment after the decision is final?

02Information & resources

Power over: Key information and resources are withheld or selectively distributed to preserve control.

Power with: People share what others need to understand the situation and contribute to the work.

A question to test this distinction

Who can see the budget, understand the constraints, and help decide what gets priority?

03Relationships

Power over: Fear, distrust, or indifference makes it costly to ask questions or challenge a decision.

Power with: People work to earn trust, show empathy, and recognize one another’s capacity to contribute.

A question to test this distinction

What happens when someone disagrees with the person who has more authority?

04Learning & accountability

Power over: A few people are treated as the source of knowledge; problems lead to blame.

Power with: People bring experience worth learning from; the group examines and improves its processes.

A question to test this distinction

Whose experience can change the group’s explanation of what went wrong?

Sharing influence does not remove limits on time, money, or responsibility. Examine which choices are open, which are constrained, and who has a say in setting those boundaries.

FICTIONAL ALTERNATIVE FOR ANY JOURNAL PROMPT

A workshop that misses the need

A campus group plans a free workshop for new students. Organizers choose the topic, time, and format before inviting participants. They welcome everyone warmly, but several participants say the time is difficult and the topic misses their main questions. The group has one room reservation and a limited budget.

Use only the information given when describing what happened. Distinguish stated effects from unknown ones, and develop your own ideas where a prompt asks for a possible change.

Prompt 1: Notice a Pattern

Thinking about leadership you have encountered across settings, where have you seen power exercised over people, built with people, or a mixture of both? Describe one pattern and use a specific feature from the reading to explain it. A leader may have a formal title or influence a group through everyday actions.

If you use the fictional case: describe a pattern it could illustrate across settings and label it as a possibility.

Prompt 2: Examine Power Over

Describe a specific moment when someone exercised power over others. What did they do, and what gave them the ability to do it?

Explain who could influence the decision and what effects you observed. Connect your interpretation to a specific feature from the reading. If an effect is uncertain, say what you would need to know.

Prompt 3: Examine Power With

Describe a specific moment when someone helped build power with others. What could the people involved actually help shape or decide?

Explain the choices that supported that influence and any effects you observed. Connect your interpretation to the reading, and name a constraint or question that remains. You may use the same leader or setting as Prompt 2 if it contains both approaches.

If you use the fictional case: propose your own power-with revision and describe the effects you would check, rather than claiming they occurred.

OPTIONAL THINKING TOOL · FOR JOURNAL PROMPT 4

Which relational practice would matter most here?

Choose a practice from Friday’s deck to explore a question about power. More than one may matter. Consider why you would start with this one and what you might need to practice yourself.

Select a practice to see a question you can use with your situation.

Selections are not saved or submitted. Keep any thinking you want to use in your journal.

Read all the practice questions
  • Care: Whose needs are you attending to, and who gets to say what those needs are?
  • Attunement: What might you be missing about how others are experiencing this situation?
  • Communication: Who can make themselves understood, and whose message is easy to overlook?
  • Collaboration: Which part of the decision can people actually shape together?
  • Trust building: What would make it possible to disagree without losing your place in the group?
  • Co-creating meaning: Whose account of the problem shapes what the group believes needs to happen?
  • Navigating tension or conflict: What disagreement needs attention before the group can make a decision?
  • Repairing ruptures or harm: Who gets to say what repair would require after harm?

Prompt 4: Change One Decision

Return to your own Week 3 space observation, Week 4 campus-collaboration idea, or a public or fictional situation. Select one decision within it. Who currently shapes that decision, and whose perspective is missing or has less influence?

Propose one feasible change that would give affected people a meaningful role in shaping the decision. Choose one relational practice from Week 4: care, attunement, communication, collaboration, trust building, co-creating meaning, navigating tension or conflict, or repairing ruptures or harm. Explain why you would start with that practice and what you would need to practice yourself to make the change possible.

Explain how affected people could decline or change your proposal, and what you would do if they did. Name a real constraint and end with one question you would ask them before proceeding. You do not need to contact anyone or carry out the proposal before class. Bring this question into our next work on learning and designing with people.
